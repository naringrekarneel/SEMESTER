# Semantic Similarity in Information Retrieval

## 1. Introduction

**Semantic similarity** measures how similar two pieces of text are based on their **meaning**, rather than simply comparing the exact words they contain.

In Information Retrieval, semantic similarity helps a system understand **what the user means**, even when the query and document use different words.

### Simple example

User searches:

> **"car repair"**

A traditional keyword system may focus on the words *car* and *repair*.

A semantic system can understand that:

> **"automobile servicing"**

has a similar meaning.

So it can retrieve the second document even though the exact words are different.

---

# 2. Semantic Similarity vs Keyword Similarity

### Keyword-based similarity

Looks mainly at matching words.

```text
Query:    "car repair"

Document: "car repair service"

→ High similarity
```

But:

```text
Query:    "car repair"

Document: "automobile servicing"

→ May receive low similarity
```

because the exact words don't match.

### Semantic similarity

Looks at the **meaning and relationships between words**.

```text
"car repair"
      ↕
"automobile servicing"

→ High semantic similarity
```

---

# 3. How Semantic Similarity Works

Modern IR systems commonly represent words, sentences, or documents as **vectors/embeddings**.

For example:

```text
car        → [0.72, 0.41, 0.19, ...]
automobile → [0.70, 0.43, 0.21, ...]
banana     → [0.05, 0.82, 0.11, ...]
```

The vectors for **car** and **automobile** are close because they have related meanings.

A similarity measure such as **cosine similarity** can then be used.

[
\text{Cosine Similarity}(A,B)
=============================

\frac{A\cdot B}{|A||B|}
]

A higher value generally indicates greater semantic similarity.

---

# 4. Methods for Measuring Semantic Similarity

## A. Word Embeddings

Models such as:

* **Word2Vec**
* **GloVe**
* **FastText**

represent words as dense vectors.

Words with related meanings tend to have similar vector representations.

Example:

```text
king ↔ queen
car ↔ automobile
doctor ↔ physician
```

---

## B. Sentence and Document Embeddings

Instead of representing individual words, an entire sentence or document can be represented as a vector.

Example:

```text
Sentence 1:
"I love programming."

Sentence 2:
"Programming is something I enjoy."

                ↓

        Similar embeddings
                ↓
       High semantic similarity
```

This is useful for comparing entire documents and queries.

---

## C. Transformer-Based Models

Modern systems use transformer-based language models to generate **context-aware representations**.

These models can better understand that a word may have different meanings depending on its context.

For example:

```text
"I deposited money in the bank."
             vs.
"I sat beside the river bank."
```

The word **bank** has different meanings in the two sentences.

Context-aware models can distinguish between them.

---

# 5. Importance of Semantic Similarity in IR

## 1. Improves Search Relevance

Semantic similarity allows the system to retrieve documents that are **conceptually related** to the query.

Example:

```text
Query:
"how to reduce electricity consumption"

Relevant document:
"ways to save energy at home"
```

Even though the words differ, their meanings are closely related.

---

## 2. Handles Synonyms

Different words can express the same idea.

```text
car ↔ automobile
doctor ↔ physician
movie ↔ film
```

Semantic similarity allows the system to recognize these relationships.

---

## 3. Handles Different Word Forms

It can recognize related concepts even when wording differs.

Example:

```text
"students learning programming"

        ↕

"learners studying computer coding"
```

A semantic system can identify the conceptual relationship.

---

## 4. Improves Query Expansion

A search engine can expand the user's query using related terms.

For example:

```text
Query:
"laptop"

        ↓

Related concepts:
notebook
computer
portable computer
```

This can increase the number of useful results retrieved.

---

## 5. Handles Natural-Language Queries

Users don't always search using perfect keywords.

Example:

> **"What are some good ways to learn Python?"**

A semantic IR system can understand that documents about:

* Python tutorials
* Python learning resources
* Python courses

may all be relevant.

---

## 6. Improves Question Answering

Semantic similarity helps match a user's question with documents containing the answer even when the wording is different.

```text
Question:
"Who created Python?"

Document:
"Python was developed by Guido van Rossum."

        ↓

Semantic match → Relevant
```

---

## 7. Helps Document Clustering

Documents with similar meanings can be grouped together.

```text
             Documents
                 ↓
       Semantic Representation
                 ↓
        ┌────────┼────────┐
        ↓        ↓        ↓
     Sports   Politics  Technology
```

This makes large document collections easier to organize.

---

# 6. Traditional vs Semantic Retrieval

| Feature                      | Traditional Keyword Retrieval | Semantic Retrieval               |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Focus**                    | Exact/related terms           | Meaning                          |
| **Synonyms**                 | Limited handling              | Better handling                  |
| **Different wording**        | May fail                      | Can recognize similarity         |
| **Representation**           | Keywords, TF-IDF              | Embeddings                       |
| **Context**                  | Limited                       | Better                           |
| **Natural-language queries** | Limited                       | Better                           |
| **Search quality**           | Good for exact queries        | Better for meaning-based queries |

---

# 7. Example in IR

Consider the query:

> **"best methods to protect computers from viruses"**

Suppose the database contains:

**Document 1:**

> "Computer virus protection techniques"

**Document 2:**

> "How to secure your PC against malware"

**Document 3:**

> "History of computer processors"

A semantic IR system would likely rank:

```text
1. Document 1 → Highly relevant
2. Document 2 → Highly relevant
3. Document 3 → Not relevant
```

Even though Document 2 uses **"secure"**, **"PC"**, and **"malware"** instead of exactly repeating the query's words, its meaning is closely related.

# 8. Semantic Similarity Pipeline

```text
       User Query
           ↓
    Query Embedding
           ↓
   ┌──────────────────┐
   │ Compare with     │
   │ Document Vectors │
   └────────┬─────────┘
            ↓
    Similarity Score
            ↓
       Rank Results
            ↓
     Relevant Documents
```

---

# 9. Advantages

* Understands meaning rather than just keywords.
* Handles synonyms and related terms.
* Improves search relevance.
* Handles variations in wording.
* Supports natural-language queries.
* Useful for semantic search and question answering.
* Helps document clustering and recommendation.

### Limitation

Semantic retrieval can be **computationally more expensive** than simple keyword matching and may sometimes consider documents semantically related even when they are not actually relevant to the user's specific intent.

---

# 10. Conclusion

**Semantic similarity is the measurement of similarity between texts based on their meaning. It is important in Information Retrieval because it allows systems to retrieve relevant documents even when the query and document use different words. Using word embeddings, document embeddings, and modern transformer-based representations, semantic similarity improves search relevance, query expansion, question answering, clustering, and overall user experience.**

### Easy memory trick

**Keyword similarity → "Do the words match?"**

**Semantic similarity → "Does the meaning match?"**
